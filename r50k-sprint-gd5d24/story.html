The R50K Sprint

Fifteen days, one operator, an AI running the plays. Written as it happens.

Day 0: the inventory

The brief was simple: R50,000 in 15 days, mostly without me. The first thing the agent did was refuse to start selling. It spent the first night working out what "R50,000" should mean.

Three readings sit under that number. Money already owed to the business, which feels like winning when it lands but isn't new. Normal store sales that would have happened anyway. And money that only exists because the sprint happened. Pick the wrong one and you can "hit" the target on day two without doing anything. So the first decision it put to me was the definition, not a tactic.

The second surprise: the biggest play on the board, a Black Friday offer to 840 past customers, was ready but blocked by our own rule. A courier partner had a data breach the week before, and we had agreed that no sales email goes out until customers have been told about it. The notices hadn't gone. So the very first move of a revenue sprint is a compliance task. It's the right order, and it costs about three days on the calendar.

By the end of the night: eight plays ranked by expected rand per minute of my time, roughly 57% of the target visible from what already exists, and nine yes/no questions waiting for the morning.

Night 1, part two: the rules change, and the build starts

Halfway through the night the brief got sharper. Only new sales count. Repeat customers are fair game. The agent builds everything, the human only presses send. And the agent never talks to a customer.

That killed the easy money (existing retainers and invoices) and made the job a build job. Within a couple of hours: the Black Friday discounts were built in the store but scheduled for next year, so they can't fire by accident, and going live is one date change. The landing page went up unpublished. Five campaign emails and a legal notice were loaded into the CRM as templates with nothing attached to send them. The customer lists were cut by consent and by whether the last order had actually arrived, and kept out of version control.

Two small things worth keeping. The shop's live pricing rules quietly changed one of the planned prices, so the agent changed the copy to match the till rather than the other way round. And the hottest-looking abandoned cart turned out to be the owner's own test order. The second-hottest, a real repeat customer, got a two-line personal note drafted for a human to send.

Night 1, part three: the customers were the leads all along

The owner pushed back, rightly: the agent had been treating 848 past customers as an email list. They are 848 businesses that have already paid us once. Sorted by what they do, 93 turned out to be guesthouses and lodges, and 49 car-related. Eleven agents fanned out: four researched every one of them (website, how bookings work, review scores, who runs it), seven built personal demos from each business's own rooms, rates, cars and photos.

By 2am there were thirteen live demos, each addressed to the person who actually bought the review stand. The research also corrected itself several times: three "no website" lodges did have websites, a "parked" dealer domain was the wrong domain, and one manager name that looked like placeholder text belonged to the real buyer. Every one of those would have been an embarrassing first line in a cold email.

Night 1, part four: the money that was already asking

The most valuable hour of the night was not building anything. It was reading 192 CRM conversations, one message at a time. Fourteen people had asked to buy and got a bot. One wanted fifteen stands. One had waited three weeks for a mockup. Two had placed bank-deposit orders that nobody chased. The "abandoned cart" the agent had drafted a recovery note for was not abandoned at all: the customer had ordered by EFT the same evening. Reading the actual conversation turned a wrong message into the right one.
