Fake Bank SMS & Email Phishing

Also called: Smishing · Bank alert scam · Fraud department SMS

A text or email that looks like it came from your bank warns of a suspicious transaction and pushes you to a cloned login page or a phone number staffed by the criminals.

Red flags

If you are looking at this right now, these are the things to check first.

  • The message contains a link. Real banks tell you to use the app you already have.
  • You are asked to phone a number printed inside the message rather than the one on your card.
  • Anyone asks you to read out, forward or type an OTP, PIN or full card number.
  • The domain is nearly right but not right: extra words, hyphens, or an unusual ending.
  • Extreme time pressure, often a countdown: 'reverse within 10 minutes or the payment clears'.
  • The greeting is generic ('Dear Client') when your bank normally uses your name.

How it works

  1. 1

    The bait lands

    You get an SMS, WhatsApp or email: a R4 999 purchase at a store you have never used, a new beneficiary added, or your profile 'locked'. It is engineered to make your stomach drop.

  2. 2

    A single urgent action

    The message gives you exactly one thing to do: click the link to reverse it, or call the number in the message. Both routes lead to the criminals, never to the bank.

  3. 3

    The cloned front door

    The link opens a pixel-accurate copy of your bank's login page on a lookalike domain. Whatever you type is relayed live to a criminal sitting on the real banking site.

  4. 4

    The OTP harvest

    The real bank sends you a genuine OTP because a real transaction is being attempted. The fake site or the friendly 'fraud consultant' asks you to read it out or type it in.

  5. 5

    The drain

    Funds move in seconds to a mule account, then to a second and third account, then out as cash or crypto. By the time the bank's fraud desk answers, the trail is cold.

What it looks like in practice

How to protect yourself

  • Treat every unexpected bank message as fake until you prove otherwise. Never click, never call the number in the message.
  • Open the bank's own app, or phone the number physically printed on your bank card, and ask them directly.
  • Memorise this: no legitimate bank employee will ever ask you for an OTP, PIN or password. There is no exception to this rule.
  • Set low transaction limits and per-day payment limits in your banking app. It costs you nothing and caps the damage.
  • Turn on in-app approvals instead of SMS OTPs where your bank offers it.

If this has already happened to you

Work down this list in order. Speed matters more than anything else here.

  1. Phone your bank's fraud line immediately, from the number on your card. Speed is the single biggest factor in recovery.
  2. Ask them to freeze the account, reverse or recall the payment, and flag the recipient account.
  3. Change your banking password and PIN from a different, clean device.
  4. Report the incident to the SAPS and get a case number. Your bank will ask for it.
  5. Report the fraudulent number or domain to your bank so it can be taken down for the next person.

Questions people ask

The SMS came in the same thread as my real bank messages. Doesn't that prove it is genuine?

No. Sender IDs are trivially spoofed, so a fraudulent message can drop into the same conversation thread as years of genuine ones. Thread position proves nothing at all.

Will my bank refund me?

Sometimes, but there is no guarantee. Banks distinguish between unauthorised fraud and transactions you were tricked into authorising yourself. Reporting within minutes materially improves your odds. If you are unhappy with the outcome you can escalate to the National Financial Ombud Scheme.

Is it safer to click the link on a phone or a computer?

Neither is safe. Phishing pages capture credentials identically on both, and phone screens hide the full URL, which makes them slightly worse.

The one rule

No bank, retailer, courier or government department will ever ask for your OTP, PIN or password. Not to verify you, not to help you, not to stop a fraud.
