Romance & Relationship Scams

Also called: Dating scam · Catfishing for money · Blesser scam

A months-long relationship is manufactured so that a series of emergencies, and eventually an investment, can be funded by you.

Red flags

If you are looking at this right now, these are the things to check first.

  • They can never video call properly, or the camera is always broken, dark or brief.
  • They profess deep feelings unusually early and message constantly.
  • They move you off the dating platform quickly.
  • Every planned meeting collapses at the last moment, often with a cost attached.
  • The first request for money is small, urgent and framed as a loan.
  • They introduce an investment platform, however casually.

How it works

  1. 1

    A profile built for you

    Photographs are stolen, usually from a real person's social media, often someone in the military, on an oil rig, or working abroad. The backstory conveniently explains why meeting is impossible.

  2. 2

    Intensity, fast

    Contact is constant and the emotional escalation is rapid. Talk of a future together comes early. The relationship moves off the dating app to WhatsApp within days, away from platform monitoring.

  3. 3

    The first small crisis

    A phone that broke, customs duty, a medical bill for a child. Small, urgent, repayable. Paying it converts you from a target into an invested participant.

  4. 4

    Escalation

    The amounts grow. So does the guilt if you hesitate. Reluctance is met with hurt rather than anger, which is far more effective.

  5. 5

    The investment ending

    Many romance scams now finish as crypto scams: your partner shares the trading platform that made them wealthy. This is the same operation, and it is where the largest losses occur.

What it looks like in practice

How to protect yourself

  • Reverse image search every profile photo. Stolen images are the most common single tell and take thirty seconds to check.
  • Insist on a live video call early. Not a recording, not a photo, a call where you can ask them to do something specific.
  • Set an absolute rule: no money to anyone you have not met in person. No exceptions, no matter the story.
  • Tell one trusted friend about the relationship. Scammers work hard to isolate victims and secrecy is a warning in itself.
  • Be alert to the crypto pivot. If a romantic partner introduces you to a trading platform, the relationship was the setup.

If this has already happened to you

Work down this list in order. Speed matters more than anything else here.

  1. Stop all payments and cut contact completely. Do not negotiate or seek an explanation.
  2. Preserve everything: chats, profiles, photos, payment records, wallet addresses.
  3. Report to your bank, to the SAPS, and to the dating platform.
  4. Expect a follow-up 'recovery' approach and refuse it. Victim lists are resold.
  5. Talk to someone. The financial loss is usually smaller than the shame, and the shame is what stops people reporting.

Questions people ask

We video called, so it must be real.

Not necessarily. Short, poor-quality calls using stolen footage or deepfake filters are now routine. Ask for something spontaneous, like holding up a specific number of fingers.

I sent money by crypto. Any chance of recovery?

Very little. Report the wallet addresses to any exchange involved, quickly, and be wary of anyone who promises recovery for a fee.

Why did I fall for this?

Because it was engineered by people who do it full time, using techniques refined against thousands of others. Susceptibility here is not a measure of intelligence.
