QR Code Scams

Also called: Quishing · Fake parking QR · Malicious QR sticker

A fraudulent QR code placed over a real one sends your payment to a criminal or your details to a phishing page.

Red flags

If you are looking at this right now, these are the things to check first.

  • A QR sticker that is misaligned, raised at the edges, or clearly stuck over something else.
  • The page it opens asks you to log into your bank rather than simply to pay.
  • The recipient name shown in your app does not match the business.
  • A QR code in an unsolicited email, SMS or letter.
  • Being asked to scan a code to 'verify' or 'release' something.

How it works

  1. 1

    A sticker over a sticker

    A fraudulent QR code is printed and placed over the legitimate one on a parking meter, restaurant table, poster or donation stand. It looks entirely native.

  2. 2

    Nothing looks wrong

    A QR code is unreadable to a human. There is no way to tell by looking whether it points where it claims to.

  3. 3

    Payment or phishing

    Either the payment goes to the criminal's wallet, or the code opens a cloned banking or payment login that harvests credentials and OTPs.

  4. 4

    Delayed discovery

    For small payments, most people never check. For phishing variants, the account compromise shows up days later.

What it looks like in practice

How to protect yourself

  • Always check the merchant name and amount shown in your app before confirming a QR payment.
  • Feel the code. A sticker over a printed surface is usually detectable with a fingernail.
  • Never log into banking through a QR code. Legitimate QR payments do not require a banking login.
  • Prefer paying inside, at the till, or through the venue's own app.
  • Be sceptical of QR codes on posters, flyers and unsolicited post.

If this has already happened to you

Work down this list in order. Speed matters more than anything else here.

  1. If you paid the wrong recipient, report to your bank immediately and request a recall.
  2. If you entered banking credentials, phone the fraud line and change your password from a clean device.
  3. Tell the business or municipality so the fraudulent sticker is removed.
  4. Open a SAPS case, particularly where a public site was tampered with.

Questions people ask

Can a QR code itself contain a virus?

Not directly. It contains a link or payment instruction. The risk lies entirely in where it sends you and what you do there.

How do I check a QR code before scanning?

Most phone cameras preview the destination address before opening it. Read that preview, and stop if the domain is not what you expect.

The one rule

No bank, retailer, courier or government department will ever ask for your OTP, PIN or password. Not to verify you, not to help you, not to stop a fraud.
