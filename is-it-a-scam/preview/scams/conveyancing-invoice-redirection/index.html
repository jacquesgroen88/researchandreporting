Property Deposit & Invoice Redirection

Also called: Business Email Compromise · BEC · Attorney trust account scam · Banking details change fraud

Criminals sit inside an email conversation between you and your attorney, estate agent or supplier, then send a genuine-looking invoice with their own bank account on it.

Red flags

If you are looking at this right now, these are the things to check first.

  • Any change of banking details, ever, for any reason. This is the single defining red flag.
  • A new email address that differs by one character from the one you have been using.
  • A request to keep the payment 'urgent and confidential' or to pay before a deadline expires.
  • An invoice sent as a PDF you did not expect, or a re-sent invoice 'because the first one had an error'.
  • Reply-to address differs from the sender address.

How it works

  1. 1

    An inbox is compromised

    Usually not yours. A mailbox at the law firm, agency or supplier is breached, often months earlier, and quietly monitored.

  2. 2

    They wait for the money moment

    The criminal reads the thread until a large payment is imminent. They know the amount, the date, the property, the names of everyone involved.

  3. 3

    The switch

    You receive what appears to be the same invoice or letter, on the right letterhead, in the right thread, with correct references. Only the bank account number has changed. Sometimes a note explains the firm has 'changed banks' or 'is auditing the old account'.

  4. 4

    You pay, correctly, to the wrong people

    Nothing about the transaction is technically fraudulent from the bank's point of view. You authorised it. You typed it in. It clears.

  5. 5

    Discovery comes days later

    The real attorney asks where the money is. By then the funds have been layered through multiple accounts and withdrawn.

What it looks like in practice

How to protect yourself

  • Establish banking details once, verbally, at the start of the relationship, and write them down yourself. Then refuse to change them under any circumstance.
  • Before any large payment, phone the recipient on a number you already had, never a number from the email, and read the account number back to them digit by digit.
  • Send a R1 test payment first and confirm receipt verbally before sending the balance.
  • Ask your attorney or supplier for their formal policy in writing that they will never change banking details by email.
  • For businesses: require dual authorisation and a callback on every banking-detail change, with no exceptions for urgency.

If this has already happened to you

Work down this list in order. Speed matters more than anything else here.

  1. Phone your bank within minutes and request an urgent recall. Under a few hours, recovery is genuinely possible. After a day, it rarely is.
  2. Notify the recipient bank's fraud department directly as well as your own.
  3. Tell the attorney or supplier immediately so they can shut down the compromised mailbox and warn their other clients.
  4. Open a SAPS case and, given the value, ask about referral to the Hawks.
  5. Speak to a lawyer about liability. Where the compromise was on the firm's side, the loss is not automatically yours to carry.

Questions people ask

The email came from the real address, in the real thread. How is that possible?

Because the criminal is logged into the real mailbox. They are not spoofing anything. This is why verification must happen on a different channel, by phone, not by replying to the email.

Who is liable, me or the attorney?

It is genuinely contested and depends on where the breach occurred and what warnings were given. South African courts have found against attorneys' firms where the compromise was on their side and they failed to warn the client adequately. Get legal advice rather than accepting the loss.

Is a phone call really enough?

Only if you dial a number you obtained independently, from a previous document, the firm's official website, or your own contacts. A number in the suspect email is worthless.

The one rule

No bank, retailer, courier or government department will ever ask for your OTP, PIN or password. Not to verify you, not to help you, not to stop a fraud.
