Identity & Account Takeover

SIM Swap Fraud

Also called: SIM hijacking · Number porting fraud

Criminals convince your mobile network to move your number onto a SIM card they hold, which hands them every OTP your bank sends.

Red flags

If you are looking at this right now, these are the things to check first.

  • Your phone suddenly loses all network signal for no reason, especially at night or over a weekend.
  • You stop receiving calls and SMSes while data over Wi-Fi still works fine.
  • An unexpected message from your network saying a SIM swap or port has been requested.
  • Friends say your number is ringing but you never see the calls.

How it works

  1. 1

    Your details are gathered first

    A SIM swap is the second half of an attack. The criminal already has your ID number, banking username and often your password, bought from a data breach or harvested by phishing.

  2. 2

    The swap is requested

    Posing as you, they ask your network to port the number to a new SIM, sometimes through a bribed or careless store agent.

  3. 3

    Your phone goes dark

    Your handset loses signal and shows 'No service' or 'SIM not provisioned'. This is the only warning you get, and it is easy to mistake for a network outage.

  4. 4

    The OTPs arrive on their phone

    Every verification code your bank sends now lands with the criminal. They log in, add beneficiaries, raise limits and pay themselves.

  5. 5

    It happens at the worst time

    Swaps are typically timed for a Friday evening or a public holiday, when branch fraud desks are thin and you are least likely to notice quickly.

What it looks like in practice

How to protect yourself

  • Move off SMS OTPs wherever you can. Use your bank's in-app approval or an authenticator app instead.
  • Ask your network to place a port-out or SIM-swap lock on your number, with a passphrase only you know.
  • Treat sudden, unexplained loss of signal as an emergency, not an inconvenience. Verify on another phone immediately.
  • Never reuse your banking password anywhere else. Most SIM swaps only work because the password was already stolen.
  • Keep a second, unpublished number or an email-based recovery route for critical accounts.

If this has already happened to you

Work down this list in order. Speed matters more than anything else here.

  1. Get to another phone and call your bank's fraud line within minutes. Freeze everything.
  2. Call your mobile network and demand the number be restored and the fraudulent SIM blocked.
  3. Change every password that used that number for recovery, starting with email.
  4. Open a SAPS case and get a case number.
  5. Ask your network in writing for the record of who requested the swap, when, and through which channel. You will need it if you dispute the loss.

Questions people ask

Can this happen without me clicking anything?

Yes. If your credentials leaked in a data breach elsewhere, the swap can be the first thing you ever notice.

Does an eSIM protect me?

Somewhat. eSIM re-provisioning usually requires more verification, but a determined attacker with your identity documents can still push it through. The stronger protection is removing SMS from your authentication entirely.

Is the network liable?

It depends on whether they followed their own verification process. Getting their written record of the swap request is the first step in any dispute.

The one rule

No bank, retailer, courier or government department will ever ask for your OTP, PIN or password. Not to verify you, not to help you, not to stop a fraud.
