Ponzi & Pyramid Schemes

Also called: Stokvel investment scheme · Money circle · Blessing loom · Gifting circle

Early participants are paid with money from later participants, not from any real trading or business, until recruitment slows and the whole structure collapses.

Red flags

If you are looking at this right now, these are the things to check first.

  • Returns depend on recruiting others, however it is dressed up.
  • Fixed high returns quoted as a certainty, for example 30% per month.
  • No comprehensible explanation of where profit actually comes from, or an explanation that dissolves under a second question.
  • Pressure to reinvest rather than withdraw.
  • Not registered with the FSCA, or registered for something unrelated to what it is selling.
  • Heavy use of lifestyle imagery, testimonies and group euphoria in place of financial documents.

How it works

  1. 1

    It arrives through someone you trust

    Not a stranger. A cousin, a colleague, a church member, a stokvel chairperson. This is what makes it work, and it is why it spreads so fast through tight communities.

  2. 2

    The early payouts are real

    The first cohort genuinely gets paid, in full, on time. They become the most credible salespeople the scheme could possibly buy, and they believe every word they are saying.

  3. 3

    Recruitment becomes the product

    Returns are explicitly or quietly tied to how many people you bring in. Whatever the stated business is, the actual income is new members' deposits.

  4. 4

    Reinvestment is encouraged

    Members are pushed to roll profits back in rather than withdraw. This delays the collapse and deepens each person's exposure.

  5. 5

    The maths runs out

    Recruitment slows, withdrawals are delayed 'due to system upgrades', then suspended, then the operators are unreachable. Everyone after roughly the first 15% loses everything.

What it looks like in practice

How to protect yourself

  • Ask one question and insist on a real answer: where does the profit come from, specifically? Not 'trading', not 'forex', but who pays it and for what.
  • Check the FSCA register. Any entity taking deposits or giving advice must be licensed.
  • Treat the fact that a friend got paid as evidence of nothing. Early payouts are the mechanism, not proof of legitimacy.
  • Never recruit family into something you have not independently verified. Your credibility is the asset being harvested.
  • Be especially careful where a scheme is wrapped around a stokvel, church group or burial society. The trust is the vulnerability.

If this has already happened to you

Work down this list in order. Speed matters more than anything else here.

  1. Stop contributing immediately and do not put in more to 'protect' what you have already paid.
  2. Report to the FSCA and the SAPS. Large schemes are also of interest to the Hawks.
  3. Preserve the WhatsApp groups, screenshots, deposit slips and member lists before groups are deleted.
  4. Warn the community you recruited from, quickly and directly. Slowing recruitment protects people below you.
  5. Where a scheme is placed under liquidation, register as a creditor. Recoveries are usually cents on the rand but they are not always zero.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between a Ponzi and a pyramid scheme?

In a Ponzi, a central operator pays 'returns' from new deposits and members are passive. In a pyramid, members must recruit to earn. Both fail for the same arithmetical reason and both are illegal in South Africa.

Is a stokvel a pyramid scheme?

No. A genuine stokvel is a rotating savings club: members pool contributions and each takes a turn receiving the pot. Nobody earns a return, and no new recruits are needed. If your 'stokvel' promises profits or requires recruitment, it is not a stokvel.

I got paid. Am I safe?

Legally you may still be exposed, particularly if you recruited others. Liquidators in South Africa have pursued net-winners to claw back payouts that were funded by later victims' money.
