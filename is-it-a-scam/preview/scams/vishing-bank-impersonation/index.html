Bank Impersonation Phone Calls

Also called: Vishing · Fraud department call · Safe account scam

A calm, professional caller claiming to be your bank's fraud department talks you through 'securing' your money, which means moving it to them.

Red flags

If you are looking at this right now, these are the things to check first.

  • Any mention of a 'safe account', 'holding account' or 'secure account'. This concept does not exist in banking.
  • A request for an OTP, PIN, password, or for you to approve something in your app while they wait.
  • A request to install remote access software of any kind.
  • They discourage you from hanging up, or stay on the line 'for your security'.
  • You are told not to discuss it with branch staff or family because the fraud investigation is confidential.
  • Being asked to withdraw cash or buy gift cards or crypto as part of an 'investigation'.

How it works

  1. 1

    They already know things about you

    Your name, your bank, the last four digits of your card, maybe a recent genuine transaction. All of it is available from breached data, and all of it is deployed in the first thirty seconds to establish authority.

  2. 2

    They put you on their side

    The framing is crucial: they are not asking you for anything, they are helping you stop a fraud that is happening right now. You and the caller are allies against a third party.

  3. 3

    Verification theatre

    They may invite you to hang up and call back, then keep the line open, or tell you to check that the number matches the one on the bank's website. Caller ID is trivially spoofed.

  4. 4

    The safe account

    The core move. Your money is 'at risk' so it must be moved to a secure holding account while your profile is rebuilt. No bank on earth does this. The safe account is theirs.

  5. 5

    Or the remote access variant

    Alternatively you are asked to install AnyDesk or TeamViewer so the 'technician' can secure your device. They then operate your banking profile directly while a black screen hides what they are doing.

What it looks like in practice

How to protect yourself

  • Adopt one unbreakable habit: end every unexpected financial call and phone back on the number on your card. A genuine bank will not object.
  • Wait five minutes and use a different phone before calling back, or dial a known contact first to confirm the line has cleared. Attackers can hold a line open.
  • Say out loud, every time: 'no bank asks for OTPs and no bank has a safe account'.
  • Agree a rule with older relatives now, before it happens: any call about money gets ended and discussed with you first.
  • Never install remote access software at the request of an inbound caller.

If this has already happened to you

Work down this list in order. Speed matters more than anything else here.

  1. Phone the real fraud line immediately from a different phone, using the number on your card.
  2. If remote software was installed, disconnect the device from the internet and uninstall it, then change passwords from a clean device.
  3. Open a SAPS case and get a case number.
  4. Ask the bank in writing for the payment destination details and whether a recall was attempted.
  5. Escalate to the National Financial Ombud Scheme if the bank declines to help and you believe their controls failed.

Questions people ask

The number on my screen was definitely my bank's real number.

Caller ID is not evidence. Spoofing a displayed number requires no special access and is used in most of these calls precisely because people trust it.

Does a bank ever ask me to move money for my own safety?

Never. If an account is compromised, banks freeze or block it. They do not ask customers to transfer funds elsewhere.

They asked me to confirm my details rather than give them. Is that safer?

No. 'Confirm' is a technique, not a safeguard. It lets the caller collect what they do not have while sounding like they already know it.

The one rule

No bank, retailer, courier or government department will ever ask for your OTP, PIN or password. Not to verify you, not to help you, not to stop a fraud.
