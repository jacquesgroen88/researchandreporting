The money model

Three cases with every assumption stated. The headline is not the total, it is the shape: forty-five enterprise accounts do 63% of the work from 7% of the customers. Any version of this plan that grows the bottom of the base does not reach seven figures.

The unit that matters

One enterprise account is worth the entire entry tier

At $1,200 a month, a single enterprise account produces $14,400 a year. The fifty-five entry accounts together produce about $625 a month, or $7,500 a year. One enterprise close is worth roughly two years of the entire volume tier. That ratio is the whole model, and it is the reason the plan reallocates effort the way it does.

The low case is not a failure case

Half a million of ARR from a base of $84k is a 6x in two years, achieved mostly by catching demand that already arrives. It is what happens if the only thing that changes is that enterprise enquiries stop falling into a shared mailbox. The difference between low and base is almost entirely whether someone is employed to close.

The assumption that could break everything

Every case above assumes the existing concentrated revenue stays. That is unverifiable today, because there is no subscription event history and no churn measurement of any kind. If one of the three large accounts leaves in year one, the base case loses roughly $12,000 to $24,000 of ARR and, worse, nobody would notice for a billing cycle. This is why the subscription event log is week one of the project plan and not a data-hygiene nicety.

The closer is the decision that separates the low case from the base case. At $1,200 average account value, one closed enterprise account a month covers a salesperson comfortably, and the base case asks for two. The hire should be gated on Q1 producing at least three qualified opportunities through the new form, which is the check written into decision gate four in 13 The Project Plan.

Doubling the entry tier from 300 to 500 accounts moves ARR by less than the difference between closing 1.5 and 2 enterprise accounts a month. The cheapest-looking growth is the least valuable growth, and it is the only growth the current systems are built to produce.

The one-line version

Getting from $84k to roughly $1m of ARR in twenty-four months requires forty-two additional enterprise accounts, which is under two a month. Everything else in this Blueprint exists to make those forty-two possible: the pipeline so enquiries are not forgotten, the routing so the form feeds it, the security pack and entity so procurement can proceed, the SLA so a contract can be signed, the certificate campaign so they arrive qualified, and the measurement so nobody has to guess which of it worked.
