Part 5 · Systems

Five systems, in the order that pays for the next one.

Starting with the one you already bought and have not used since January. Nothing here gets automated before it works by hand, because automating a broken process only produces the same mess faster and with less visibility.

Prepared for Jade Mann · The Acreage + Good & Proper · 15 August 2026

Three rules for the order

1. What you already own comes first. The review engine is a tool you bought in November, that produced thirteen reviews in December, and that has not run since January. Restarting it costs nothing and is the highest return per rand in this document, because the rand was already spent.

2. Measurement before automation. The second system exists to produce the four numbers in Part 2. Until those exist, every later system is being built on an opinion about what is slow.

3. Manual first, always. Every system below runs by hand for at least two weeks before anything is automated. If a rule cannot survive a person following it, it will not survive a machine following it, and the machine will be much harder to debug.

The review engine

You are rated 4.7 against a market leader on 4.4, on a twentieth of their evidence, and the evidence stopped accumulating in January.

What it does

Asks for a review at the two moments a person is most likely to give one, and makes the ask easy enough that they actually do it there and then rather than intending to later.

The two moments

A
At the point of payment, in the restaurant
This is the one that ran in December and produced thirteen reviews. It is a tap on the stand you already own. It stopped, and nobody decided to stop it. Restarting it is a conversation with the floor team, not a project.
R0 new spendFloor team
B
After a function, to the organiser and the guests
This one does not exist at all today, and it is worth more per send than the restaurant ask. A function puts between 50 and 3,000 people on the property who have just had an experience, and the organiser in particular has just had a stressful thing go well. That is the most positive they will ever be about you, and you currently ask them for nothing.
NewPhase 2

Replies are part of the system, not an afterthought

Seven of your last forty reviews have a reply. Replies are public, permanent, free, and read by every future customer who scrolls. They are also the only part of your reputation you control directly. The rule is simple: every review gets a reply, negative ones within a day.

Why this is first

Reviews are the slowest lever in the entire Blueprint and the one with the longest tail. Something started in August shows up in October. Everything else here is faster, which is exactly why this one cannot wait for them.

And the window is closing on its own: 34 more five-star reviews move you from 4.7 to 4.8. For Accolades to move from 4.4 to 4.5 would take 296. You can still move your number and the market leader effectively cannot move theirs — until the day they start collecting properly.

2count
Phase 1 · the foundation · all findings

Enquiry capture and response

The least interesting system in this document and the one everything else is judged by. Every leak in Part 1 is sized against an enquiry count that does not currently exist anywhere.

What it does

Puts every enquiry from every channel into one place, timestamps it, records where it came from and what happened to it, and holds someone to a stated response time.

What gets recorded, per enquiry

  • Date and time it arrived, and the channel it came through
  • Occasion, rough guest count, and the date they are asking about
  • Time of first human reply — this is the number that becomes the response-time metric
  • Outcome: booked, lost, or still open, and if lost, one word for why
Start this in a spreadsheet and do not apologise for it.

A shared sheet that gets filled in every day beats a CRM that gets abandoned in week three, and it is honest about the fact that nobody yet knows what the columns should be. Once four weeks of real data exist, the right tool becomes obvious rather than a guess. Outgrowing the spreadsheet is a good problem and it is not this quarter's.

The response side

An immediate automatic acknowledgement that confirms the date and guest count back to them and attaches the function pack, then a human reply within the stated time. The automatic one is not the answer, it is the receipt — and it is the thing they forward to the second decision-maker while they wait for you.

The decision this system exists to force

At Gate 1 it answers whether your constraint is reach or follow-up. If enquiries are few but convert well, the plan pushes on reach. If enquiries arrive and die, more traffic makes things worse and the whole second half of the roadmap changes. Nobody can answer that today, including us.

Phase 2 · needs the website and system 2

The booking flow

The path from an enquiry to money in the account, with a named owner at every step and nothing that depends on somebody remembering.

The steps

1
Enquiry arrives and is acknowledged automatically
Receipt with the pack attached, within seconds.
2
Qualified against date, guest count and occasion
This is where the sub-80 routing rule fires. See system 4.
3
Availability confirmed against one calendar
One calendar, not a calendar per person. Double bookings are a reputation event, not an admin event.
4
Quote sent, from a template, with the bundled catering option on it
Every quote carries the venue-plus-catering number, because that is the offer nobody else in Midrand can make.
5
Follow-up sequence if there is no answer
Two or three touches over two weeks. This is where most venue revenue quietly dies, because a quote sent is treated as a job finished.
6
Deposit, confirmation, and the run-up
Then the handover to whoever runs the day, and the post-function review ask from system 1.
Step 5 is the one to watch.

In almost every venue we have seen, the biggest single recoverable number is not more enquiries, it is quotes that were sent once and never followed up. We cannot tell you whether that is true here, because there is no record of it. System 2 will tell us within a month, and if it is true, this step alone changes more than the website does.

4route
Phase 2 · Finding 4

Cross-referral routing

Two businesses that answer the same question at the same moment, and currently make the buyer find each one separately.

The three rules

  • Under the Good & Proper minimum goes to The Acreage. Under 80 guests, or under 150 from October to December, is currently a polite no. It is also a perfectly good venue booking. The reply becomes an introduction rather than a decline.
  • Every venue enquiry gets the catering option quoted. Not offered as an afterthought at the end — priced into the quote as a single number from the start.
  • Every Good & Proper enquiry without a venue gets asked whether they have one. If they do not, you own one. This is one question added to an existing conversation.
Why this is a system and not a good intention.

Cross-referral is the kind of thing everyone agrees with in a meeting and nobody does on a Tuesday. It needs a named owner, a written rule, and a count. Gate 2 in Part 2 tests it with one blunt question: did a single referral happen in either direction? Zero means the rule was written and never used, which is a management problem rather than a marketing one, and it is cheaper to find that out in October than in March.

The number that sizes it

Two figures, both countable from your own records in an afternoon: functions at the venue last year without Good & Proper catering, and Good & Proper events last year at a venue that was not yours. Until they exist this is a mechanism. After they exist it is very likely the cheapest money in the Blueprint, because both businesses already have the customer.

5assist
Phase 3 · needs your email samples

The AI email assistant, for Good & Proper

Drafts the replies you could write in your sleep, so a person can check and send them in seconds instead of writing them again.

What it does, precisely

Reads an incoming enquiry, drafts a reply in your voice using your actual answers to the questions that repeat, and puts it in front of a human. It handles the recurring ground: minimums, what the per-head price includes, how the deposit works, whether you cover a given area, what happens if it rains.

What it does not do

  • It does not send anything on its own. Every draft is checked by a person.
  • It does not quote a bespoke event or negotiate a price.
  • It does not touch anything after a deposit is paid.
  • It does not talk to a customer who is unhappy.
Why it is last, and why that is not a demotion.

An assistant trained before the voice work in Part 3 and before the enquiry data in system 2 would learn to write the replies you send today, at the speed you send them today. The value is in it writing the replies you want to be sending, to a standard you have decided on. Building it first would be faster and would automate the wrong thing permanently.

What it needs from you

A sample of real Good & Proper enquiry threads, ideally twenty or more, including the awkward ones. The awkward ones are the valuable ones: they are where the judgement lives that has to be either captured or deliberately excluded.

Scope

What we are not building, and when to revisit.

  • A CRM. Revisit when the spreadsheet has more than about 40 live enquiries at once. Before that it is overhead pretending to be progress.
  • A website chatbot. Revisit once the site exists and the enquiry data shows what people actually ask. A bot built before then answers questions nobody had.
  • Online booking and payment for functions. Functions are consultative and a booking engine would fight the sales conversation. The restaurant is a different case and a much simpler one.
  • An assistant on The Acreage side. The venue's enquiries are more varied and higher value than Good & Proper's, so the repeatable ground is thinner and the cost of a wrong answer is higher. Revisit after the Good & Proper one has been running for a quarter.
  • Stock, staff or supplier systems. Out of scope for this Blueprint entirely. Mentioned only so it is clear it was considered and set aside, not missed.
The edges of this document

What Part 5 assumes.

  • Assumption That the review stand is still on site and working. We know it was bought in November and that December produced thirteen reviews. We do not know where it is now.
  • Assumption That there is one person who can own the enquiry count. Every system here degrades to nothing without a named owner. If there is nobody, that is the finding, and it changes what is realistic.
  • Assumption That enquiries currently arrive across several channels. Phone, Facebook, email, walk-in. If they in fact all arrive in one inbox already, system 2 is much smaller than described.
  • Assumption That Good & Proper's enquiry email volume justifies an assistant at all. Below roughly ten repetitive threads a week it is not worth building, and we would tell you so rather than build it.
