Part 3 · Brand & Voice

Two businesses, two voices, and one of them is already written.

Sixty-eight people have described The Acreage in public, in their own words, and they agree with each other. That is the brand voice. The work is not inventing one, it is noticing the one you have and using it everywhere else.

Prepared for Jade Mann · The Acreage + Good & Proper · 15 August 2026

Why this document does not start with a mood board

Most brand work starts by asking the owner how they want to sound, and the answer is almost always "premium, warm and professional", which describes nothing and rules out no one. We had a better source available: your reviews. Sixty-eight people, unprompted, unpaid, describing what it is actually like to be at The Acreage.

So the voice below is not a proposal about who you could be. It is a description of who your customers already say you are, written down so that your website, your Google listing and your enquiry replies can stop sounding like a different business to the one people are reviewing.

Good & Proper is the opposite case, and is handled differently and second. It sells to a different person, for a different reason, and we have far less evidence about it.

Business one

The Acreage

The position, in one sentence

The Acreage is not a hall you hire. It is a working racecourse twenty minutes from Sandton, where the staff get remembered by name.

Both halves of that are defensible and neither is currently being said. The racecourse cannot be copied, discounted against, or opened next door: over a thousand horses stabled, and it is presently mentioned in passing on a Facebook page. The staff are named in your reviews, repeatedly, which is rare enough to be a genuine asset rather than a nice line.

The evidence this is built on Observed

Pulled from your Google Business Profile and its review history on 13 August 2026. These are your customers' words, not ours.

  • Reviewers name individual staff: Queen, Prince, Andrew. People do not name staff at venues they found adequate.
  • Recurring images: warm blankets, the racecourse view, a get-away from the city buzz, family time.
  • Google's own auto-generated tags on your listing: welcoming staff on five reviews, joyful smile on two, plus pet friendly and horse racetrack view.
  • The food that gets named: cheesecake, steak sandwich, great coffee. Specific dishes, not "the food was lovely".
The one thing this evidence does not contain.

None of the 68 reviews mentions a wedding, and only two mention corporate events. Your reputation is a genuinely excellent breakfast-and-view restaurant, and that is the wrong reputation for the half of the business that carries the revenue. The voice below is written to be true to the restaurant experience while pointing it at functions, because the warmth people describe is exactly what a function buyer is trying to buy. It is not a different personality. It is the same one, applied to a bigger occasion.

Four voice principles

1. Name the place, not the package.

Your competitors sell rooms and rates. You have something none of them can put in a brochure. Lead with the racecourse, the lawn, the view and the twenty minutes from Sandton. The capacity and the inclusions follow, they do not open.

2. Warm, not formal.

Your reviewers write "a get-away from the city buzz". They do not write "a premier event destination". If your website sounds more corporate than your customers do, it reads as a different business and the warmth stops being believable before anyone visits.

3. Specific beats superlative, every time.

"Over a thousand horses stabled on the property" is a fact that makes a person picture something. "A stunning setting" is a claim every venue in Midrand makes and none of them can prove. The specific version also survives being forwarded to a sceptical finance approver, which the superlative does not.

4. Put a number on it.

From 50 to 3,000 guests. Twenty minutes from Sandton. Kitchen until whatever the real time is. A business that publishes no figures forces every single conversation to begin with a question, and most people do not ask the question. They leave.

The vocabulary

Use

  • the racecourse, the lawn, the terrace
  • twenty minutes from Sandton
  • from 50 to 3,000
  • blankets, the view, the horses
  • Queen, Prince, Andrew: name your people
  • a morning, an evening, a Saturday
  • what it costs, what it includes

Avoid

  • premier, world-class, bespoke
  • nestled, boasting, boasts
  • unforgettable, magical, dream
  • your special day
  • we pride ourselves on
  • state-of-the-art facilities
  • enquire for pricing
The test for any sentence.

Could a competitor in Midrand put their name on it and have it still be true? If yes, it is doing no work. Nine of the ten words in the right-hand column fail that test, which is why they are there.

Three rewrites

The same information, in the voice above. The "before" column is drawn from what is currently published or from the standard formulation the venue market uses.

Before

A premier event venue and restaurant in Midrand, offering a stunning setting for weddings, corporate functions and special occasions. Enquire today.

After

The first reply to a function enquiry
Before

Thank you for your enquiry. We will get right back to you.

After

Thanks, we have your enquiry for [date], [number] guests. You will have a proper answer from a person within [X business hours], including whether that date is still open and what it would cost. In the meantime, here is the function pack, which is the thing to forward to whoever else has to say yes.

Before

Visual language

One rule that matters more than the palette itself.

Gold on green is your signature and it is also the least legible pair in the set. Use it for the logo lockup and short display lines only. Body text in gold on green fails to read for anyone over about forty, on a phone, outdoors, which describes a meaningful share of the people deciding whether to book a venue.

Type

A serif for headings and a plain sans for everything else. The concepts built in Part 4 use Playfair Display SC with Karla in the warm direction. The serif carries the racecourse and the age of the place; the sans keeps capacities, prices and directions readable. Both are free and web-safe, so nothing here creates a licensing cost.

This is a proposal to react to, not a decision. If your logo's wordmark has a typeface with it, that overrides this and we build around it instead.

Photography, the largest gap in the brand Observed

Your entire image library contains no people. Every photograph we have been given or found shows an empty room, an empty lawn, a plate, or a view. This is not a small aesthetic issue. You are selling occasions, and every picture of the occasion is a picture of nobody having it.

It also directly contradicts your reviews, which are almost entirely about people: staff, family, warmth, being looked after. The photography says empty; the reviews say full. The reviews are right.

Three usable images were supplied on 6 and 7 August, a function toast, a corporate team day and a market day, and they immediately became the strongest things in both website concepts. That is the evidence for commissioning more.

The shot list, if a shoot happens

  • A full lawn. Long tables, people seated, the track behind them. This is the single most valuable photograph you do not have.
  • An evening. Everything published shows daylight, which quietly reinforces the 15:30 closing time Google is already telling people about.
  • A staff portrait, with names. Your reviewers already name them. Give the names faces.
  • A corporate day mid-session. The year-end market needs to see itself, not a wedding.
  • The blankets. Small, specific, repeatedly mentioned, and currently invisible.
  • The horses, with people watching them. The asset no competitor has, with a human in frame so the scale reads.

Assumption That commissioning is possible at all. If it is not, the fallback is a structured programme of collecting guest and organiser photographs with permission, which is slower and less controlled but costs nothing. This is one of the five decisions in Part 2.

Business two

Good & Proper Food Trucks

The position, in one sentence

Good & Proper does not sell food. It sells the certainty that nothing goes wrong in front of your colleagues.

The buyer here is not a bride and is not dreaming. It is an office manager or an events coordinator whose name is on the decision, and whose actual fear is standing in front of 200 colleagues when the catering fails. Fifteen years of experience and a network of 100 to 150 vendors is not a credential to list at the bottom of a page. It is the entire product.

What is currently published Observed

Read from goodandproperfoodtrucks.co.za on 15 August 2026.

  • Headline: "Food Truck Catering For Unforgettable Events".
  • Promise: "We'll handle everything from set up to strike".
  • Credential: "15 years of food truck and event catering management experience".
  • Terms: minimum 80 guests, rising to 150 in October, November and December. A single price per head with no figure attached. 50% deposit, balance two weeks before.
  • Enquiry form: Name, Email, Phone, Company (required), Number of guests. Submit reads "We'll get right back to you".

Four voice principles

1. Competence, not charm.

"We'll handle everything from set up to strike" is the best sentence on the site and it is doing the right job. "Unforgettable" in the headline is doing none: it is a word every caterer uses and no buyer believes. This buyer is de-risking a decision, and confidence reads as competence far better than enthusiasm does.

2. Lead with the constraints, do not bury them.

The 80-guest minimum and the December jump to 150 are qualifiers, and qualifiers stated early save both sides a fortnight. Put them in the first screen. A small enquiry that self-selects out has cost you nothing; a small enquiry that gets three emails deep and then hits a wall has cost you an hour and produced a bad feeling about the brand.

3. Every promise carries a number.

"We'll get right back to you" cannot be kept or broken. "We reply within one business hour" can be both, which is exactly why it is worth saying: it is a commitment, and it is the only claim on the page a competitor would have to match rather than merely repeat.

4. The single price per head is the differentiator, so say what is in it.

Most catering quotes are a fight about line items, and everyone booking one knows it. One price per head is genuinely better and it is currently stated without a figure and without inclusions, which converts your strongest commercial idea into another vague claim. What it includes matters more than what it is.

Two rewrites

The headline
Before

Food Truck Catering For Unforgettable Events

After

Food truck catering for corporate events, from 80 guests up. One price per head, fifteen years of it, and we handle everything from set up to strike.

The enquiry form
Before

Name · Email · Phone · Company (required) · Number of guests → "We'll get right back to you"

After

Name · Email · Phone · Number of guests · Date · Company (optional) → "We reply within one business hour, in business hours." Plus a line before the form: "We cater from 80 guests, and from 150 in October to December."

Why Company should stop being mandatory.

A required Company field turns away every private, family and non-corporate enquiry at the last step, silently and with no record that it happened. Some of those are genuinely too small, which the guest minimum already handles more politely. Others are exactly the sub-80 enquiries that should be arriving at The Acreage instead of vanishing. You cannot route what you never captured.

Visual language: held open, on purpose

We have not been given a brand pack for Good & Proper, and we are not going to invent one and present it as a finding. What we can set now is the one structural decision that matters, which is how the two brands relate to each other.

Keep them visually distinct. Do not extend The Acreage's forest and gold across Good & Proper. The cross-referral in Part 2 is worth more if it reads as two capable businesses that work together than as one business wearing two names. A corporate buyer who suspects the "recommended caterer" is just the venue upselling itself discounts the recommendation. Two identities, one visible relationship, and the endorsement carries weight in both directions.

What we need to finish this section: logo files and any existing brand guide, the fonts currently used on the site, and whether the trucks themselves carry a livery that the brand should be built around. That last one usually decides it, because the trucks are the most-seen asset the business owns.

The edges of this document

What Part 3 assumes.

  • Assumption That the review language reflects functions and not only the restaurant. The reviews we can read are overwhelmingly restaurant visits. We have assumed the warmth carries into functions, which is likely but unevidenced. If function clients experience something different, the voice needs a second source.
  • Assumption That the three supporting colours are open. Paper, Ink and Sage are our proposal. If you have an existing brand guide with its own neutrals, it wins and we rebuild around it.
  • Assumption That Good & Proper's corporate-only focus is a choice rather than a limitation. Their FAQ states they do not cater weddings. Whether that is capability, capacity or preference changes the whole cross-referral design, and it is a question on the kickoff call.
  • Assumption That both businesses stay separately branded. If you intend to merge them commercially, most of the second half of this document changes.
