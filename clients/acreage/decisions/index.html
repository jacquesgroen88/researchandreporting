How the Photographs Were Decided

A record of how every photograph on the site was chosen, cropped, changed and in some cases changed back, across four versions. Written after Jade found that photographs she had settled on had moved, which was a fair thing to find and worth explaining properly.

The short version

If you read nothing else

The site looked good on a computer and was quietly poor on a phone. We found that by measuring every photograph on every screen size rather than looking at a laptop, fixed it, and in fixing it changed more photographs than we should have without asking first. Twenty six of those changes have now been put back.

The mistake worth naming: we shipped a version with forty photograph changes in it and did not send the list of what had changed. Jade had to find them. The list should have arrived with the version, not after it.

How a photograph gets decided

The method, in order
  1. Measure before judging. Every image on the site is loaded at three screen widths and we record what fraction of each photograph actually survives the crop, and whether it is being stretched past its own resolution. Opinions about photographs are worth having after that, not before.
  2. Then look at it. The measurement finds what is broken. It cannot tell you whether a picture is welcoming. Every cut is rendered at the exact size it appears on the page and looked at. More than once the measurement said a crop had improved and the eye said it had not.
  3. Cut the photograph for the slot it goes in. A tall photograph in a wide slot loses its top and bottom whatever you do. The fix is a file cut to that slot's shape, not a different photograph.
  4. Never enlarge. If the original cannot fill the file we need, we write the largest honest size and record that we did. A soft photograph is worse than a tight one.
  5. Leave what the client chose alone. Where Jade named a photograph, it stays, and we check the actual file rather than assuming.

The phone column is the point. In the version Jade reviewed, a visitor on a phone was seeing about two thirds of each photograph on average, and on the big sliders closer to half. Nothing was broken, no image failed to load, which is why it had gone unnoticed through six versions.

Why a phone needed its own photographs

The one technical thing worth understanding

A photograph has a shape and so does the space it goes into. When they disagree, the browser crops the photograph to fit, from the middle outwards.

The big sliders are a wide band on a computer and a nearly square box on a phone. The photographs are cut wide. So on a phone the browser was throwing away about half the width of every one of them, and whatever the photographer had put near the left and right edges simply was not there.

The fix is to give a phone its own cut of the same photograph, taken around the same subject. Not a different picture. This is the part that caused the confusion: a photograph that is byte for byte the one Jade approved can still look different on her phone, because her phone is now being handed a differently shaped cut of it.

We got this wrong in how we reported it. We told Jacques that Jade's chosen photographs were untouched, and checked the files to prove it. That was true of the files and not true of what she sees. The right check is what the client's device is served, not what is in the folder.

Jacques has since asked for four of those photographs to have no phone cut at all, so that the ones Jade named look identical on every screen. That costs some cropping on a phone and is a deliberate trade.

Us

  • Every crop and every shape decision, with the method above.
  • Twelve photographs swapped on our own judgement in v13 under the welcoming brief. Eleven of those were ours alone, and that is the batch that caused the problem. Fifteen of the twenty six restorations are from it.

What we would do differently

The rules this produced
  • Ship the change list with the version. Any version that moves a photograph the client has already seen goes out with a before-and-after list attached. Not afterwards.
  • Separate the two kinds of change. Fixing how a photograph is cropped and choosing a different photograph are different acts and need different permission. We ran them together.
  • A brief to improve is not permission to replace. Where a photograph the client has seen is merely weak rather than broken, propose it, do not swap it.
  • Check what the device is served, not what is in the folder.
  • Measure, then look. Both. A crop that measures better can look worse, and one of Jade's own photographs proved it.
