1. No bulk pricing, no bulk page, no bulk enquiry route

The evidence. Fifty-five orders worth R2,500 or more have produced R299,458, about 31% of lifetime revenue. Every one of those was placed through a retail checkout designed for a single owner-operator buying one stand. There is no published volume tier, no minimum-quantity break, no "ordering for multiple locations" path, and no quote request. Customers wanting ten units either work it out themselves or leave.

What it costs. Three separate behaviours prove the loss. Customers split single intentions into multiple orders (one hotel group buyer placed three orders in a single day). A ten-stand cart worth R5,115 was abandoned outright. And the two largest orders in the store's history, R20,411 and R20,285, had to be processed as draft orders outside the normal flow, which means they were manual rescues rather than a working process.

Costing basis

Conservatively, half of the R2,500-plus cohort would have bought more units if a ladder had existed, at an average uplift of R1,200 per order. Across roughly 2.3 bulk orders a month at current volume that is about R3,300. The larger component is the orders that never happen: on the assumption that a published bulk route converts even two additional group orders a month at the cohort's R5,400 average, that is R10,800, plus the recovery of split and abandoned bulk carts. Estimated R25,000 per month, and this is the most conservative number on the page.

2. Franchise and group demand never escalated to head office

The evidence, re-counted against the full order book on 9 August. A national beauty franchise has bought eight times, across eight different branches, from six buyer addresses, four of them on the group's own corporate domain. A global attractions franchise has bought four times across three cities. A national optical franchise has bought for three stores through two buyers. A national retail services franchise has bought for three stores. A national estate agency franchise, two national QSR brands, a restaurant franchise, a laundromat group, a fuel forecourt operator and two multi-brand franchisees make up the rest. A large restaurant group made contact on 4 August about roughly 23 sites. Fourteen groups. In every case the buyer was a location, and in no case was the group ever contacted.

What it costs. a national beauty franchise's four orders totalled roughly R8,300. a national beauty franchise operates around 200 salons. Even a 25% group rollout at the current custom-stand price is in the region of R32,000 of hardware plus a recurring system contract. That is one brand, and it is a brand that has already bought four times and proved the product internally without any help.

The compounding version of this leak

Every franchisee order that ships without a head-office conversation is a customer and a reference and a case study being spent on a single transaction. The proof is generated and then thrown away.

3. The Smart Business Card is suppressed in paid search

The evidence. nfc business card appears in the search terms report with 6 impressions, 2 clicks and a 33.33% click-through rate, marked EXCLUDED. The Custom Smart Business Card is simultaneously the highest revenue-per-order product in the catalogue at R2,423 and the highest units-per-order at 4.0. It took twelve orders in twelve months.

What it costs. The negative keyword was almost certainly added when the account was focused purely on review devices and generic business-card traffic looked irrelevant. It is now blocking the entry point to the best product. At the account's demonstrated non-brand return of roughly 7x and even a modest R1,500 of monthly spend against this cluster, the arithmetic gets to roughly R10,500 of revenue per month before counting the higher basket size, which is why the estimate on the table is R12,000.

Fix time: under five minutes. This is the single highest ratio of money to effort in the entire Blueprint.

4. The courier fee is killing exactly the carts worth winning

The evidence. A 57.9% checkout drop has been attributed to the R125 courier fee. A ten-stand cart worth R5,115 was abandoned. Roughly fifty checkouts a month are abandoned, worth about R40,000.

The constraint we are respecting. The decision not to change shipping generally stands and is not reopened here. On a R424 single-card order, a R125 fee is 29% of the order and giving it away is real margin.

The scoped fix

Free delivery above R2,500 only. On a R2,500 order the R125 fee is 5% of value against a a high gross margin, so it is nearly free to absorb. More usefully it becomes a reason to add units: a customer at R1,900 is now told they are R600 from free delivery, which is one more stand. This converts the single largest checkout leak into the bulk ladder's top step without touching the single-unit decision at all. Estimated R9,000 per month from recovered bulk carts and threshold-driven basket growth.

5. Google Search is underfunded at roughly 7x

The evidence. Non-brand search returned 7.04x and the blended account 13.07x, on total spend of R534.90 across thirty days against a budget of roughly R108 a day that should have delivered around R3,240. Meanwhile Meta prospecting runs at roughly 1.1x to 1.3x in-account on many multiples of that spend.

The honest caveat. A conversion double-count was only corrected on 9 July, so part of that window's value is inflated. Halve it and non-brand is still above 3.5x, which is still the best channel in the business by a wide margin.

What it costs. Scaling non-brand to a real R200 a day and accepting a conservative 3x rather than 7x produces roughly R18,000 of monthly revenue on about R6,000 of spend. That number rises once the bulk and smart-card keyword clusters are actually live, because those queries carry a much larger basket.

6. No reorder or expansion motion on proven buyers

The evidence. Reorders are already happening entirely unprompted. A professional services firm bought six Smart Business Cards then four more a month later. A boutique guesthouse bought seven stands then seven more. A coastal tourism operator placed five orders across three months. Nobody triggered any of it.

What it costs. The store has roughly 700 unique buyers. If unprompted reorder behaviour is visible in this many accounts without any prompting at all, a structured 60-day and 180-day expansion sequence against the bulk cohort and multi-order buyers is low-risk. At a 4% monthly reactivation rate across the buyer base at the R1,099 average order value, that is roughly R14,000 per month against near-zero marginal cost.

Hard rule before any of this sends

Every outreach list must be deduped against completed Shopify orders first. Roughly one in four apparent "abandoned" carts actually completed. One buyer abandoned three times and then completed order #1778 thirteen minutes after the third attempt. Sending him a recovery email would have made the business look like it does not know its own customers.

7. The pipeline is stalled

The evidence. Roughly R381,000 of open value across about 158 opportunities in the Reviewtap System. Around thirty named GROWTH (R5,999/mo) and SCALE (R9,500/mo) upsell deals have sat untouched for nine to twenty-four days or more. Named multi-site prospects inside that pipeline include a national attraction, a professional sports union, a game lodge, a funeral group, an aesthetic clinic group, a vehicle solutions company at R15,000, and a national QSR group.

What it costs. Closing two mid-tier deals out of thirty adds roughly R15,500 of monthly recurring revenue. Recurring revenue is worth a multiple of its annual value, so this is the single most valuable leak on the page measured in enterprise value rather than in cash.

8. Abandoned checkouts with no systematic recovery

Roughly fifty abandoned checkouts a month, worth about R40,000. Industry-standard recovery of 10% to 15% would return R4,000 to R6,000 monthly. The standing decision holds: recovery link and a human nudge only, no discount and no free-shipping re-pitch on single units. The larger carts get a personal email rather than an automated one, because a R5,115 cart is a sales conversation, not an email flow.

9. No organic presence against live demand

The evidence. The search terms report shows entire impression pools being seen and never clicked, because there is no page to send them to. nfc stand (13 impressions, zero clicks), google reviews for business (13, zero), google reviews card (13, zero), nfc stands (10, zero), google review link generator (8, zero), google review device (5, zero).

Alongside that sits a fully-formed question cluster with no content behind it: how to create a QR code for Google reviews, how do Google review cards work, how to set up a Google review card, how to ask customers to review your business, how to get more Google reviews.

What it costs. Paid search is currently renting traffic that the site could own. This is the slowest fix in the document and the one with the longest compounding tail. Full architecture in doc 07. Estimated R20,000 per month at maturity, six to nine months out.

10. No reseller or white-label rail

The evidence. An agency put three custom stands in a cart. Two marketing agencies, a media agency and a niche agency all appear in the order history. These are firms buying for clients, one small order at a time.

The market precedent. Every international peer that scaled did it through partners. NFC Tagify runs dropship and white-label packaging with no minimum order. wCard.io is reseller-first, giving partners their own domain, logo and pricing control. a white-label operator runs published volume breaks from 10% at ten units to 40% at a hundred. QRCodeChimp lets partners brand and price the whole thing themselves.

What it costs. At a very low landed cost per unit, ReviewTap can offer partner pricing that looks generous and still clears a high margin. Six active partners each moving eight units a month at partner pricing is roughly R18,000 per month of revenue that requires no advertising spend at all.

Why this is a leak and not a nitpick

Fixing this is an afternoon and it protects every high-value conversation that follows.
