LinkedIn strategy: the only channel where the group buyer is addressable by name

Meta sells one stand to one owner. LinkedIn is the only platform where a Franchise Marketing Manager, a Group Operations Director or a Dealer Principal can be reached individually, by job title, at the company you have already decided to sell to. This document answers the question that was actually asked — personal page or business page — and then sets out exactly what gets posted, where, and how often.

Written 10 August 2026 in response to doc 15 feedback. Assumes the group motion in doc 10 and the named-account list in it.

The answer, up front

Where you post

Your personal profile is the engine. The ReviewTap page is a shopfront you keep tidy and mostly ignore.

Roughly 80% of effort goes to your personal profile, 20% to the ReviewTap company page, and the 20% is largely reposting and keeping it from looking abandoned.

This is not a preference. LinkedIn's feed demotes company-page content heavily — a personal profile with a few hundred connections routinely outreaches a company page with several thousand followers, because the platform is built to put people in front of people. Posting group-programme content on the ReviewTap page and expecting reach is the single most common way businesses waste a year on LinkedIn.

The one reason the company page matters more than the reach numbers suggest

ReviewTap is being positioned for sale. A company page with two years of consistent, credible activity is an asset that transfers with the business. Your personal profile does not transfer — it walks out with you. So the company page is not a reach play, it is an asset-building play, and the correct amount of effort is "enough that a buyer doing diligence sees a living brand", not "enough to generate leads".

Your profile, before you post anything

Posting into a profile that reads like a CV wastes the reach. Four fixes, about an hour.

  1. Headline. Not "Founder at ReviewTap". Something that names the buyer's problem: "I help multi-site groups see what every location actually rates — and fix the ones dragging the brand down". The headline follows every comment you leave anywhere on the platform, so it is the most-read line you own.
  2. About section. First two lines are all most people read. Lead with the finding, not the biography: the spread between a group's best and worst location, and the fact that nobody at head office can see it.
  3. Featured section. Pin three things: the SA Review Benchmark, the group rating dashboard, and one case study. This is where a curious buyer goes after a good post, and it is currently empty.
  4. Banner. One line: what you do, for whom. Not a logo on a gradient.

The unfair advantage, and it is sitting unused

You now hold live rating and review data for any South African group you choose to look at, and it takes about twenty minutes to produce. That is an endless supply of pillar-2 posts that no competitor can copy, because they do not have the tooling or the benchmark to compare against. One audit is a post, a proposal and a sales conversation at the same time.

Cadence

Three posts a week beats seven. Consistency matters more than volume, and the failure mode here is a burst for two weeks followed by nothing.

Post between 07:00 and 09:00 SAST on weekdays, which is when this audience is on the platform. Weekend posts do less volume but reach founders and owners disproportionately.

The rule that matters more than the schedule

Spend fifteen minutes a day commenting on other people's posts, thoughtfully, in your ICP's feed. Not "great post". A genuine addition, three or four lines. This is how a small profile grows without paid reach, and it puts your headline in front of the exact people you want, repeatedly, without you posting anything at all. If you only do one thing on LinkedIn, do this rather than posting.

Format rules

  • No external links in the post body. The feed suppresses them. Put the link in the first comment and say "link below".
  • First two lines are everything. Everything after "…see more" is only read by people the opening already convinced.
  • Short paragraphs, one idea each. On mobile a five-line paragraph is a wall.
  • One image beats a carousel for data posts. A dashboard screenshot with one number highlighted outperforms a designed graphic.
  • No hashtag spam. Three at most, and only if genuinely relevant.
  • Reply to every comment in the first hour. Early engagement determines distribution more than anything else you control.
  • Never post the same thing to the personal profile and the company page on the same day. Repost to the company page 24 to 48 hours later.

The first 30 days

Week 1 · Fix the profile, then start commenting

Headline, About, Featured, banner. Then connect with 20 people a week from the ICP titles above, with a note that references something specific rather than a pitch. Start the 15-minutes-a-day commenting habit immediately, before you post anything.

Week 2 · Publish the benchmark

The SA Review Benchmark is the strongest opening asset you have. Lead with the counter-intuitive finding — a 5.0 is usually a small sample, not excellence — because it earns attention from people who think they already understand reviews. Link in the first comment.

Week 3 · The first anonymised group audit

Post the shape of the Hussar Grill audit without naming them: 28 sites, a 0.5-star spread, one site frozen at 4.2, one closed site still listed. End with the question that does the selling: "do you know what your worst location rates?"

Week 4 · Convert the attention

Offer the free group audit explicitly, once. By this point three posts have demonstrated you can do it. Anyone who has engaged with the audit posts gets a direct message referencing what they engaged with — never a cold template.

What not to do

  • Do not run LinkedIn ads yet. Doc 09 carries a R150/day test with a defined kill point, and that is the right size, but organic proof should come first. Ads amplify a message that works; they do not find one.
  • Do not post product photos. A card on a counter means nothing to a group marketing manager. The estate-level problem does.
  • Do not automate connection requests or DMs. This audience is small, senior and talks to each other. Getting flagged as a spammer in the SA franchise community is a one-way door.
  • Do not post the same content to Facebook and LinkedIn. Different buyer, different register. The Facebook audience is the single-site owner; LinkedIn is head office.
  • Do not measure followers. Measure conversations started with named-account buyers. Doc 10's list of ten groups is the scoreboard.
