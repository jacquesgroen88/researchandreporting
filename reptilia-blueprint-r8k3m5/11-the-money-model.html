The money model

Every assumption behind every number in this blueprint, written down so you can disagree with it. Where a judgement was needed it was set low on purpose, so the total is a floor.

The baseline

Everything is sized against the trailing twelve months, 13 August 2025 to 13 August 2026, not against the five-year lifetime. Lifetime figures flatter the model, because the store has grown, and the question is what happens next year rather than what happened in 2022.

Line by line

1. Lapsed customers — R31,688

Population: 443 customers, last order more than 92 days ago, lifetime spend R300 or more. Combined historic revenue R644,466. Sum of their individual average order values: R396,102.

Arithmetic: R396,102 × 8% = R31,688. Low case 4%, high case 15%.

Why 8%: published reactivation rates for a warm ecommerce list run 5% to 20%. 8% sits at the low end because this list has never been mailed, consent is thin, and 207 of the 443 have been gone more than two years.

Weakness: this is largely one-time. Repeating the campaign next year against the same list yields much less. It becomes an annuity only once the reorder reminder stops people reaching the lapsed list in the first place.

2. Livestock pages — R27,390

Population: 133 live animals listed, 98 never sold a single unit, R178,945 of shelf value across those 98, average price R1,826.

Arithmetic: 15 × R1,826 = R27,390. Low case 8 animals, high case 25.

Why 15: roughly 15% of the never-sold stock moving in a year, once the pages carry sex, age, morph, feeding history, permit position, transport terms and three photographs. Livestock demand is proven — 190 orders at an AOV of R1,213 — so this is unlocking existing demand, not creating it.

Weakness: assumes the animals listed are actually available. Given that 1,481 website-flagged products show zero stock on hand, some of the 98 may be listings for animals that are long gone. This line needs a stock check before it is banked.

3. Dead URLs — R18,005

Measurement: every line item in the last twelve months was tested against the live catalogue by product ID and by name. R45,012 — 29.8% of TTM line revenue — came through products with no reachable URL today.

Arithmetic: R45,012 × 40% = R18,005. Low case 25%, high case 55%.

Why 40%: a redirect recovers the customer who arrived on a broken link and would otherwise leave. It does not recover people who would have found the product through search or navigation anyway, and it should not recover products that were discontinued deliberately.

What was NOT used: the observed post-launch drop of R589/day to R364/day. That is a 38% fall which annualises to about R82,000 — but it rests on seven days and six orders, which is far too small to carry a number. It is quoted as direction only.

Arithmetic: 140 new customers a year × (38.7% − 12.3%) × 50% recovery × R700 per recovered order = R12,916. Low case 25% recovery, high case 75%.

Why 140: 2025 produced 161 new customers and 2026 is on pace for about 190. 140 is deliberately below both.

Why this line is the most understated in the model: it prices one extra order per recovered customer. A recovered Feeder orders every 30 days at R589 to R700 — worth R7,000 or more over a year, not R700. Priced at true lifetime value this line dwarfs everything else in the document. It is stated at one order so the total does not depend on a compounding assumption.

5. Catalogue gap — R11,449

Arithmetic: 467 dead URLs carry R572,432 of lifetime revenue across roughly five years, so about R114,486 a year of historic run rate. Taken at 10% = R11,449. Low case 5%, high case 20%.

Why so low: this overlaps line 3 heavily and is discounted to a quarter weight to avoid double counting. Only products with a proven sales history are included at all.

Not in this number: the 39 products priced below their POS price, site cheaper in every case (a Small Animal Cage at R530 against R2,850). That is a live margin leak of unknown size, excluded because cost data is not available.

6. Reviews — R5,763 softest line

Arithmetic: R192,099 × 3% relative conversion lift = R5,763. Low case 1%, high case 6%.

Why it is the softest: without GA4 access there is no conversion rate to lift, so this is an industry-shaped estimate rather than a measurement. It is included because the qualitative case is overwhelming — competitors show 920+ reviews, Reptilia sells live animals at up to R14,495 with no social proof at all — but the number should be treated as illustrative.

7. Cross-sell and attach — R4,433

Trailing twelve months: 152 feed orders (27.0% ice pack attach), 33 cage orders (57.6% accessory attach), 45 livestock orders (28.9% attach any equipment).

Arithmetic: ice pack attach to 40% at R25 each, plus cage attach +15 points at R250, plus livestock attach +15 points at R400. Low case +8 points, high +22.

Why it looks small: the TTM order counts are low, so the arithmetic is small. The five-year attach picture is worse than the TTM one (13.6% ice pack lifetime against 27% now), which means attach is already improving on its own — the prompt just makes it deliberate.

8. Failed checkouts — R1,483

Arithmetic: 21 cancelled or failed orders in the last twelve months × 10% recovery × R706 = R1,483.

Why so small: because the problem largely fixed itself. The failure rate ran 17% to 24% from 2023 to 2025 and is 5.4% in 2026. Lifetime the number is much larger — 181 orders worth R124,632, with 107 email addresses that never converted at all.

The finding is worth more than the money: a 23.8% to 5.4% drop is a large, valuable, unexplained improvement. It should be identified and written down so a future change does not silently undo it.

Assumption: 50% of near-miss orders top up to clear the threshold. Even at that generous rate every level is negative, because the shipping given away on orders that already clear the bar exceeds the uplift.

What would change it: gross profit rather than revenue. If R160 does not cover Reptilia's courier cost, the forgone revenue is matched by forgone cost. That number is not in the data. Carried at zero pending one answer from Willem.

This corrects recommendation 4 of the 13 August ecommerce scorecard, which proposed a threshold at about R750 without modelling the giveaway.

What is deliberately excluded

Six things not in the total
  • Traffic and conversion rate. GA4 and GTM are firing but under an account JCE cannot access. We can measure what buyers did, not how many arrived and left. Almost certainly the largest number missing from this document.
  • Site speed. 3.21 seconds to first byte against a healthy sub-800ms. Real conversion cost, unsizable without analytics.
  • Search and navigation. Scored 2/10 and known to be the biggest conversion blocker. "lizard" returns zero results on a reptile shop. Unsizable without traffic data.
  • Margin. Everything here is revenue. Cost of goods is not in WooCommerce, so nothing has been converted to profit.
  • Paid advertising. No channel is running, and building demand before fixing the shop would be paying to send people to 404s.
  • The physical shop. Twenty years, a 4.6 Google rating, an exhibition and a petting zoo. Real assets, no way to size them from order data.

How to argue with this model

Four places it could reasonably be wrong, listed so they can be checked rather than defended:

  1. The 8% reactivation rate. If the list is colder than assumed, line 1 halves. It is also the largest line, so this is the single biggest sensitivity. It is testable within two weeks by mailing the 75-person 6-to-12-month band and measuring.
  2. The 98 never-sold animals may not exist. If half those listings are for animals already sold in the shop, line 2 halves. One stock check answers it.
  3. The 40% redirect recovery rate. Some of the 1,591 dead URLs were deliberately removed products that should stay dead. Willem's answer to decision 3 in document 10 settles this.
  4. The cohort decline may be a market effect, not a Reptilia effect. If South African reptile keeping has simply become more competitive, some of line 4 is unrecoverable. Nothing in the data can separate these, and the fix — building a retention engine — is worth doing under either explanation.
