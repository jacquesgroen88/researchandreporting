2. The concentration problem hiding at number 2

Read the buyers column

The second-biggest product in the store has two customers, and one of them is 97% of it

Montego Monty & Me Essential Adult 40kg: R18,759 across 27 orders — but only two distinct buyers. One of them, Bianca Jansen Van Vuuren, accounts for R18,199 of it across 26 orders. She is also the store's single largest customer overall at R46,403, and she stopped ordering in August 2025. Product number two in the entire catalogue is, in reality, one relationship — and that relationship has already lapsed.

The same pattern is on the Marmoset Porridge (R13,652 from 18 buyers, 95 units) and JUAVA Adult Dog 40kg (R4,564 from 3 buyers). Bulk dry food is a small number of large, regular, probably semi-trade relationships, not retail. It should be managed like an account book, not merchandised like a shelf. See document 04, ICP 4.

3. The two engines

Once the legacy line items are classified back into categories, the shape of the business is clear. Reptilia runs on two engines that behave completely differently.

"Accessories & other" includes R75,872 of line revenue that cannot be attributed to any surviving category, mostly one-off livestock records deleted when the animal sold. The real Livestock figure is therefore higher than R121,351, not lower.

Engine 1: consumables

Frozen feed + live feed + substrate = R156,561. Low value, high frequency, and the entire reason anybody comes back. Crickets alone: 466 units, 225 orders, 126 distinct buyers, 43% of whom bought them more than once — the highest repeat rate of any product in the store.

Median gap between a customer's orders: 30 days. This engine runs on a clock and nothing in the business is set to that clock.

Engine 2: hardware and animals

Livestock + housing + heating = R227,569. High value, low frequency. An order containing a live animal averages R1,213 against the R731 store average. A cage order averages R1,297.

But 190 livestock orders in five years is roughly one every ten days, and the repeat rate on a livestock-acquired customer is only 16.2%. This engine makes the money on the day; it does not make the customer.

The strategic read

Engine 2 pays the bills. Engine 1 owns the customer. The single most valuable move available is to connect them: every animal and every cage sold should start a consumable relationship, because that customer now owns something that has to eat every week for the next fifteen years. Today only 26% of livestock orders include any feed at all, and nothing follows up afterwards.

Three things fall out of this:

  • Live feed is the best acquisition product in the business. Cheapest first order (R589), highest repeat rate (40.9%), and second-highest lifetime value (R1,587). Somebody who starts with crickets is worth 2.7 times more than somebody who starts with a bag of dog food.
  • Livestock is a bad acquisition product and a great basket. R845 first order but only 16.2% ever return. Someone buys a bearded dragon and then buys its food somewhere else — which is exactly the leak that the attach work in document 09 is designed to close.
  • Cages are the worst of all: 7.4% repeat. The single largest first order in the store (R1,502) produces the customer least likely to ever come back. A cage buyer is a person setting up a habitat who then disappears, and there is no reason for that other than nobody asked them to stay.

The clearest single revenue idea in the data

Ice Pack pairs with every frozen product, and only 13.6% of feed orders include one

Ice Pack is already the store's most widely-bought item by headcount after crickets — 157 units across 77 orders from 53 different people — and it appears in six of the top twelve co-purchase pairs. Nobody merchandises it. Nobody prompts it. It has simply been bought by customers who worked out for themselves that frozen rodents need one.

Across all five years, 70 of 516 frozen or live feed orders included an ice pack (13.6%). It has improved to 27% in the last twelve months without anyone doing anything, which says the demand is there and unprompted. Every remaining order is a parcel arriving warmer than it should have.

This is not only revenue. It is the refund rate. Five refunds in five years is remarkable given that 446 frozen orders a year ship without a cold pack, and it will not hold forever.

61 products out of 832 do half the work. That is a normal, healthy Pareto — and it means the content work in this blueprint is a bounded, finishable job, not a 929-product backlog. Write 61 product pages properly and you have covered half the revenue of the business.

The other side of it

606 of 929 live products have never sold one unit. The worst concentrations are Reptile Livestock (83), Snakes (71), Dog Toys (46), Cat Toys (29) and Fish Accessories (27). Some of that is normal — a shop lists things that move slowly. But 98 of 133 live animals never selling, with R200,560 of shelf value on them, is not a slow-moving tail. It is a category with no product pages, covered in document 07.

7. Basket shape

What a typical order looks like

  • 2.96 lines per order, median 2
  • 37.2% are single-line orders
  • 7.74 units per order (feed is bought in bulk)
  • Median order total R532
  • Shipping R135 average, R160 flat rate
  • 0 coupons ever created, in five years

Where the value sits

  • Under R250 — 133 orders, R22,621
  • R250–R500 — 344 orders, R125,142
  • R500–R1,000 — 305 orders, R220,732
  • R1,000–R2,500 — 188 orders, R268,661
  • R2,500–R5,000 — 27 orders, R86,475
  • R5,000+ — 1 order, R5,820

The R500–R2,500 middle carries 67% of all revenue off 49% of orders.

The middle basket is the business. It is also the band that vanished after the 5 August relaunch — orders between R500 and R1,000 went from 0.35 a day to zero in the following week. Everything in document 09 is aimed at that band, and the reason the free-shipping threshold does not work is that it targets the wrong end of it.
